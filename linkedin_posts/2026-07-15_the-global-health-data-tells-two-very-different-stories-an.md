The global health data tells two very different stories — and both demand our attention.

The United States spends $15,474 per person on healthcare annually — 18% of GDP, the highest in the world. Yet 27 million Americans remain uninsured, and the US ranks last among high-income nations in overall health system performance. More spending doesn't automatically mean better outcomes.

Meanwhile, Sub-Saharan Africa faces a crisis of a different scale:
📍 454 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births — 25x higher than the US rate of 17.9
📍 Just 2 doctors per 10,000 people, vs. 26 in the US — a 13-fold gap
📍 943,000 trained health workers unemployed, yet a projected shortage of 5.85 million by 2030

That last point is worth sitting with. Nearly 1 million qualified health professionals are unemployed on a continent that desperately needs them. This isn't just a funding problem — it's a structural and policy challenge.

Globally, the UHC Service Coverage Index sits at 71/100, but progress has stalled since 2015. And 2.1 billion people still face catastrophic health spending each year.

The bright spot? Technology. 71% of US hospitals now use predictive AI, and 65% of individuals access patient portals. If these tools can be made accessible and affordable in resource-limited settings, they could be transformative.

The question isn't whether we have the data to act — we do. The question is whether we have the will.
