## Content

The migration story of 2025-2026 is one of the most dramatic data shifts I've tracked as an analyst -- and the numbers demand attention.

Let's start with the US: Net international migration peaked at 2.7M in 2024, but the Census Bureau projects a staggering 88% collapse to just 321,000 by 2026. For the first time in half a century, the US may see negative net migration. The foreign-born population already dropped by 1.4M in just six months (Jan-Jun 2025). The macroeconomic ripple? Consumer spending could weaken by $60-110B over 2025-2026, and sustainable monthly job growth may turn negative.

Now flip to Africa -- a completely different narrative. Remittance inflows hit $104B in 2024, double the continent's official development aid. Egypt leads at $22.7B, Nigeria at $19.8B, and Kenya's remittances now surpass tourism and agriculture as its top foreign exchange source. Yet sending money to Africa still costs 8.2% on average -- nearly 3x the UN's SDG target of 3%. That's billions left on the table.

Globally, UNHCR reports 117.3M forcibly displaced people at mid-2025 -- but here's the nuance: this is actually the first decline in a decade, driven by 6.9M returns (3x the rate of H1 2024). Intra-African migration grew 25% since 2015 to 15M people, mostly labor-driven. Meanwhile, global refugee resettlement dropped by two-thirds in 2025.

The data tells us migration isn't slowing -- it's restructuring. Capital flows, labor markets, and demographic pipelines are all being rewired simultaneously.
