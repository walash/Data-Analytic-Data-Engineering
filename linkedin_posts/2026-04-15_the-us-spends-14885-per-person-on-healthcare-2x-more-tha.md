## Content

The US spends $14,885 per person on healthcare — 2x more than any peer nation. Yet it ranks LAST in life expectancy among high-income countries.

I analyzed global healthcare cost trends vs patient outcomes across 15 countries, and the data reveals a striking paradox:

📊 THE COST-OUTCOME DISCONNECT:
→ Japan achieves 84.7 years life expectancy at $5,790/capita — 61% less than the US, with 5.4 MORE years of life
→ South Korea delivers the best cost efficiency: 84.3 years at just $4,797/capita
→ Sub-Saharan Africa spends ~$120/capita — a 125x gap vs the US, but only a 21-year outcome gap
→ US maternal mortality (18.6/100K) is 3.6x higher than the peer average of 5.1

💡 KEY INSIGHT: Spending more does NOT equal better outcomes. The data shows a logarithmic relationship — after ~$5,000/capita, additional spending yields diminishing returns. What matters more: system design, universal access, and preventive care investment.

The US is the ONLY high-income nation without universal healthcare, yet spends 17.2% of GDP on health — nearly double the OECD average. Meanwhile, countries like Spain ($5,346/capita) achieve 83.7 years life expectancy with 64% less spending.

For Sub-Saharan Africa, the challenge is different: with healthcare spending at 1/10th the global average, even modest investment increases could dramatically improve outcomes.

This analysis reinforces why healthcare economics needs more data-driven decision making. The $12.4T global healthcare market demands analysts who can identify where every dollar creates the most impact.

What's your take — should healthcare policy prioritize spending efficiency over spending volume?
