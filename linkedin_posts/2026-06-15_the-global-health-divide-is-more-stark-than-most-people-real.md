## Content

The global health divide is more stark than most people realize -- and the data tells a sobering story.

The US spends $14,885 per person on healthcare annually. Sub-Saharan Africa? Just $79. That's a 188x gap. Yet the US gains only 6 extra years of life expectancy over the global average (79 vs 73 years). Diminishing returns at scale.

What truly breaks the pattern is maternal mortality. Africa's rate stands at 454 deaths per 100,000 live births -- 45x higher than the US (10) and 3x the global average (152). This is one of the most preventable tragedies in global health today.

Here's the paradox that surprised me most: the US and Sub-Saharan Africa have nearly identical healthcare workforce densities -- 27 vs 26.8 workers per 10,000 people. The gap isn't in headcount. It's in infrastructure, equipment, and funding. Africa has the people. It needs the resources.

On the digital front, there's real momentum. 71% of US hospitals now use predictive AI in their EHRs. Meanwhile, Africa's 416 million mobile internet users are enabling a mobile-first healthcare revolution -- leapfrogging legacy infrastructure with mHealth apps for maternal care and HIV management.

The takeaway? Global health inequity is fundamentally a funding and infrastructure problem, not a knowledge or workforce one. And that's actually good news -- because those are solvable.

Data analysts and health economists have a critical role to play in turning these numbers into policy action.
