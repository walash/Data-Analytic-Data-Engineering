## Content

The global unemployment rate hit a historic low of 5.0% in 2024 — but that headline masks a deeply uneven story.

In the US, unemployment sits at 4.2% (June 2026), yet 1.9 million Americans have been jobless for 6+ months — a 27% rise year-over-year. Labor force participation has slipped to 61.5%, and payroll growth slowed to just 57,000 jobs in June. The numbers look stable on the surface, but the cracks are widening underneath.

Africa tells a different story entirely. South Africa's unemployment rate stands at 31.4% — 7.5× the US rate. Northern Africa's youth unemployment is 22.3%, and nearly 1 in 3 young people there are NEET (not in employment, education, or training). The continent adds 10–12 million young people to its labor market every year, but only creates ~3 million formal jobs. That's a structural gap of 7–9 million annually.

Sub-Saharan Africa's official unemployment rate (~5.8%) looks deceptively low — because 90% of employed youth work informally, often below the $2.15/day poverty line. The real crisis isn't unemployment; it's underemployment and working poverty.

Globally, the ILO has already downgraded its 2025 employment forecast by 7 million jobs due to trade disruptions and geopolitical tensions. And with 1 in 4 workers in roles exposed to generative AI automation, the labor market transformation ahead is unlike anything we've seen.

The data is clear: we need smarter workforce analytics, better skills-to-demand matching, and policies that go beyond headline rates.
