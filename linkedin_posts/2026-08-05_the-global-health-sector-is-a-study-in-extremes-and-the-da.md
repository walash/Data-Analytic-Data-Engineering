## Content

The global health sector is a study in extremes — and the data tells a story that every data professional should understand.

The US spent $15,474 per person on healthcare in 2024, pushing total national health expenditure to $5.3 trillion — 18% of GDP. Hospital care alone hit $1.63 trillion. Meanwhile, across Africa, two-thirds of people report going without needed medical care at least once a year due to cost or supply shortages.

Here's the paradox that keeps me thinking:

Africa has 5.72 million health workers — yet the region meets only 46% of its workforce needs. At the same time, 943,000 trained health professionals are unemployed or underemployed. That's not a talent shortage. That's a systems and financing failure.

The mortality gap is stark. Africa's maternal mortality ratio stands at 442 deaths per 100,000 live births — 2.2× the global average of 197, and more than 13× the US rate. Under-five deaths globally reached 4.9 million in 2024, with Sub-Saharan Africa absorbing 58% of that burden.

But here's where the opportunity lies:

Africa's pharmaceutical market reached $33.9B in late 2024 and is projected to hit $28–30B by 2028 (continent-specific). The global medicine market is on track for $2.4 trillion by 2029, with oncology alone at $252B. As high-income markets saturate, capital and innovation will increasingly flow toward underserved regions.

Data analytics is at the center of this shift — from optimizing supply chains to modeling disease burden and directing health investment where it matters most.
